# Flooding

> **Level 04 · Ethernet & Switching** — CCNP Enterprise (ENCOR 350-401)

---

## 1. Overview

**Flooding** is a Layer-2 switch forwarding behavior in which a frame is sent out multiple ports within the **same VLAN** when the switch does not have a specific forwarding entry for the destination, or when the frame is a broadcast or otherwise treated as flooded multicast.

Flooding is a normal and necessary part of Ethernet switching, but excessive flooding—especially during a Layer-2 loop—can contribute to a **broadcast storm** and severe network instability.

---

## 2. Core Concepts

### 2.1 When Does a Switch Flood?

A switch can flood frames in several situations:

- **Unknown unicast** — the destination MAC address is not present in the MAC address table.
    
- **Broadcast** — destination MAC is `FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF`; broadcasts are flooded throughout the VLAN.
    
- **Multicast** — multicast traffic may be flooded when the switch does not have multicast-forwarding state or multicast-aware features such as IGMP snooping constrain the forwarding.
    
- **MAC-table aging** — when a learned MAC entry ages out, traffic to that destination can become unknown unicast and be flooded again.
    
- **Initial communication** — when a switch has not yet learned the destination MAC, the first frame may be flooded.
    

### 2.2 Known Unicast vs. Flooding

A switch normally performs **unicast forwarding** when the destination MAC is known:

```text
Known unicast:
Host A → Switch → Port toward Host B
```

If the destination MAC is unknown:

```text
Unknown unicast:
Host A → Switch → multiple ports in VLAN
```

The switch still learns the **source MAC address** from the incoming frame, which helps future traffic become known unicast.

---

## 3. Flooding Scope

Flooding is limited to the **Layer-2 broadcast domain**, normally represented by a VLAN.

### Example

```text
             VLAN 10
        ┌────────────────┐
        │    Switch      │
        └────────────────┘
          │      │      │
        Host A  Host B  Host C
```

If Host A sends an unknown-unicast frame in VLAN 10:

```text
Host A → Switch
           ├──→ Host B
           └──→ Host C
```

The switch does **not** flood that frame into VLAN 20.

### Important rules

- Flooding occurs **within the applicable VLAN**.
    
- The frame is **not sent back out the ingress port**.
    
- Ports that are not members of the VLAN do not receive the flooded frame.
    
- STP-blocked/ineligible Layer-2 paths are not used for normal data forwarding.
    
- A trunk can carry flooded traffic for a VLAN to another switch, provided the VLAN is allowed and the port is forwarding for that VLAN.

## 4. Broadcast vs. Unknown-Unicast Flooding

|Feature|Broadcast|Unknown Unicast|
|---|---|---|
|Destination|`FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF`|Specific MAC address|
|MAC table required?|No|Destination must be missing/unknown|
|Flooding|Yes|Yes|
|Typical examples|ARP, DHCP Discover|Traffic to an aged/new MAC|
|Scope|VLAN/broadcast domain|VLAN/broadcast domain|
|Stops when destination is learned?|No — broadcasts remain broadcasts|Yes — becomes known unicast|
|Main concern|Broadcast storms|Excessive unknown-unicast traffic|

### DHCP Note

A DHCP client may initially use a Layer-2 broadcast because it does not yet know its DHCP server.

When a **DHCP relay** is configured, the router/L3 switch can receive the broadcast and forward the DHCP request toward the server as a routed/unicast transaction.

## 5. MAC Learning and Flooding

Flooding is closely related to **MAC address learning**.

When a switch receives a frame, it learns the **source MAC address** on the ingress interface and associates it with the VLAN.

```text
Frame:
Source MAC      = AAAA.AAAA.AAAA
Destination MAC = BBBB.BBBB.BBBB

        ↓

Switch learns:

AAAA.AAAA.AAAA → Gi0/1, VLAN 10
```

If `BBBB.BBBB.BBBB` is not in the MAC address table, the switch floods the frame within VLAN 10.

After the destination device sends traffic back:

```text
BBBB.BBBB.BBBB → Gi0/2, VLAN 10
```

the switch can subsequently forward traffic directly between the two ports.

## 6. Why Flooding Matters in Network Design

### 6.1 Normal Flooding

A small amount of flooding is expected.

Examples:

- First communication with a newly discovered host
    
- ARP requests
    
- DHCP broadcasts
    
- Certain multicast traffic
    

This is normal Ethernet behavior.

### 6.2 Excessive Flooding

Excessive flooding can consume:

- switch forwarding resources;
    
- link bandwidth;
    
- CPU resources on some devices;
    
- host processing capacity.
    

A large Layer-2 broadcast domain generally means more devices receive broadcast traffic.

### 6.3 Broadcast Storms

A **Layer-2 loop** can cause broadcast and flooded traffic to circulate repeatedly.

```text
        ┌──────── SW1 ────────┐
        │                     │
        │                     │
       SW2 ───────────────── SW3
```

Without an effective loop-prevention mechanism, a flooded frame can repeatedly traverse the Layer-2 topology.

This can result in:

- rapidly increasing traffic;
    
- MAC-table instability;
    
- high switch CPU utilization;
    
- packet loss;
    
- network-wide connectivity problems.
    

**STP/RSTP/MST** prevents normal Layer-2 loops by placing redundant paths into a non-forwarding state.

---

## 7. Multicast Flooding

Multicast requires special consideration.

Without multicast-aware forwarding information, a Layer-2 switch may treat multicast traffic similarly to flooded traffic and send it to multiple ports in the VLAN.

**IGMP snooping** allows a switch to learn which ports have interested multicast receivers and constrain multicast forwarding accordingly.

```text
Without multicast awareness:

Multicast Source
       │
     Switch
   ┌───┼───┐
   ↓   ↓   ↓
  H1  H2  H3
```

With appropriate IGMP snooping state:

```text
Multicast Source
       │
     Switch
       │
       ↓
      H2
```

The exact forwarding behavior depends on the multicast type, switch platform, and configured multicast features.

## 8. Configuration / Verification

### 8.1 View the MAC Address Table

```cisco
show mac address-table
```

This is the primary command for checking learned MAC addresses.

### 8.2 View Interface Counters

```cisco
show interfaces counters
```

Additional interface-specific commands can be used to investigate abnormal traffic or errors.

### 8.3 View a Specific VLAN

```cisco
show mac address-table vlan 10
```

### 8.4 View a Specific MAC Address

```cisco
show mac address-table address 001b.21aa.bbcc
```

### 8.5 Static MAC Entry

A static MAC entry can be configured when there is a specific design reason to prevent normal MAC learning/aging for that address:

```cisco
mac address-table static 001b.21aa.bbcc vlan 10 interface GigabitEthernet0/1
```

> **Note:** Static MAC entries should be used deliberately. They are not a general replacement for normal dynamic MAC learning.

## 9. Reducing Unnecessary Flooding

Common design/control mechanisms include:

### VLAN Segmentation

Smaller VLANs reduce the size of the Layer-2 broadcast domain.

```text
Large VLAN
   ↓
Many hosts receive broadcasts

Multiple VLANs
   ↓
Broadcast traffic is contained within each VLAN
```

### MAC Learning

Normal dynamic MAC learning reduces unknown-unicast flooding after the switch learns the destination location.

### Static MAC Entries

Useful for specific devices or security/design requirements, but should not be used unnecessarily.

### IGMP Snooping

Helps constrain multicast traffic to ports with interested receivers.

### Unknown-Unicast Suppression

Some Cisco platforms and other vendors provide platform-specific mechanisms to control or suppress excessive unknown-unicast flooding.

> **Important:** The exact command and behavior are platform/software dependent. Do not memorize a generic command such as `unknown-unicast-flooding suppressed` as a universal Cisco IOS command.

## 10. Flooding vs. Forwarding

|Traffic Type|Destination Known?|Switch Behavior|
|---|--:|---|
|Known Unicast|Yes|Forward toward destination port|
|Unknown Unicast|No|Flood within VLAN|
|Broadcast|N/A|Flood within VLAN|
|Multicast|Depends on multicast state|May be flooded or constrained|

## 11. Exam Traps

### Trap 1 — "Flooding only means broadcast"

**Incorrect.**

Unknown unicast can also be flooded, and multicast may be flooded depending on multicast forwarding state/features.

---

### Trap 2 — "Flooding goes out every switch port"

**Incorrect.**

Flooding is constrained by:

- VLAN membership;
    
- ingress-port exclusion;
    
- STP forwarding state;
    
- trunk VLAN allowance;
    
- other forwarding controls/features.
    

---

### Trap 3 — "Flooding crosses VLANs"

**Incorrect.**

A Layer-2 flooded frame remains within its VLAN.

To communicate between VLANs, traffic must cross a **Layer-3 boundary**.

---

### Trap 4 — "A MAC-table entry aging out breaks connectivity"

**Not necessarily.**

If the destination MAC is no longer known, the switch can initially flood the frame. Once the destination MAC is learned again, normal known-unicast forwarding resumes.

---

### Trap 5 — "Faster MAC aging reduces flooding"

**Generally incorrect.**

A shorter aging time can cause MAC entries to disappear sooner, which can actually increase unknown-unicast flooding.

---

### Trap 6 — "STP blocks all flooded traffic"

**Incorrect.**

STP prevents Layer-2 loops by controlling which ports are allowed to forward. Flooding still occurs over the **active forwarding topology**.

---

## 12. Troubleshooting Flooding

If excessive flooding is suspected, investigate:

```text
1. Check the MAC address table
        ↓
2. Identify unknown/unstable MAC addresses
        ↓
3. Check VLAN membership and trunk configuration
        ↓
4. Check STP topology and port states
        ↓
5. Check for Layer-2 loops
        ↓
6. Check broadcast/multicast traffic levels
        ↓
7. Check interface counters and errors
        ↓
8. Investigate MAC flapping or topology changes
```

Useful commands include:

```cisco
show mac address-table
show mac address-table dynamic
show mac address-table vlan 10
show spanning-tree
show interfaces counters
show interfaces status
```

## 13. Quick Review

|Item|Behavior|
|---|---|
|**Unknown Unicast**|Flooded within the VLAN|
|**Broadcast**|Flooded within the VLAN|
|**Multicast**|May be flooded unless constrained by multicast-aware forwarding|
|**Known Unicast**|Forwarded toward the learned destination port|
|**Ingress Port**|Not flooded back out|
|**VLAN Boundary**|Flooding does not cross it|
|**STP Blocking**|Non-forwarding paths are not used for normal data forwarding|
|**MAC Aging**|Can cause previously known traffic to become unknown unicast|
|**Main Benefit of MAC Learning**|Reduces unnecessary unknown-unicast flooding|
|**Broadcast Storm Risk**|Layer-2 loops can cause excessive flooded traffic|
|**Reduce Broadcast Domain**|VLAN segmentation|
|**Reduce Multicast Flooding**|IGMP snooping / multicast-aware features|
|**Primary Verification**|`show mac address-table`|

## 14. One-Minute Exam Summary

> **Flooding = sending a frame to multiple eligible ports within the same VLAN.**

Remember:

```text
Known Unicast
    ↓
Forward to destination port

Unknown Unicast
    ↓
Flood within VLAN

Broadcast
    ↓
Flood within VLAN

Multicast
    ↓
May flood unless multicast-aware forwarding constrains it
```

**Core rule:**

> **Flooding is a Layer-2 behavior confined to a VLAN, excludes the ingress port, and operates only across eligible forwarding paths.**
