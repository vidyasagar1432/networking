# Forwarding

> **Level 04 · Ethernet & Switching** — CCNP Enterprise (ENCOR 350-401)

---

## 1. Overview

**Forwarding** is the Layer-2 switch operation of deciding **where a received Ethernet frame should be sent** based primarily on its **destination MAC address** and the VLAN.

A simplified Layer-2 switching pipeline is:

```text
Receive frame
     ↓
Learn source MAC
     ↓
Look up destination MAC + VLAN
     ↓
┌───────────────┬───────────────┬───────────────┐
│ Known unicast │ Unknown       │ Broadcast /   │
│               │ unicast       │ multicast*    │
↓               ↓               ↓
Forward         Flood           Flood / constrain
```

* Multicast behavior depends on multicast forwarding state and features such as IGMP snooping.

---

## 2. Core Concepts

### 2.1 The Forwarding Decision

For each received frame, a switch generally performs these operations:

### Step 1 — Receive the frame

The switch receives the Ethernet frame on an ingress interface and associates the frame with the appropriate VLAN.

### Step 2 — Learn the source MAC

The switch learns the **source MAC address** on the ingress interface within that VLAN.

```text
Source MAC → Ingress Interface + VLAN
```

Example:

```text
AAAA.AAAA.AAAA → Gi0/1, VLAN 10
```

### Step 3 — Look up the destination MAC

The switch searches its MAC address table using the **destination MAC + VLAN**.

There are three important outcomes:

|Destination State|Switch Action|
|---|---|
|Known on another forwarding port|Forward out that port|
|Known on ingress port|Filter/drop|
|Unknown|Flood within the VLAN|

### Step 4 — Handle broadcast/multicast

- **Broadcast** → flooded within the VLAN.
    
- **Unknown unicast** → flooded within the VLAN.
    
- **Multicast** → may be flooded or constrained by multicast-aware forwarding such as IGMP snooping.
    

### Step 5 — Transmit

The switch sends the frame through the appropriate egress interface, applying the appropriate Layer-2 encapsulation/tagging for that link.

---

## 3. Known-Unicast Forwarding

When the destination MAC is known:

```text
          VLAN 10

Host A ── Gi0/1
             │
          ┌──┴───┐
          │ SW1  │
          └──┬───┘
             │
           Gi0/2
             │
           Host B
```

If the MAC table contains:

```text
BBBB.BBBB.BBBB → Gi0/2, VLAN 10
```

then:

```text
Host A
  ↓
SW1
  ↓
Gi0/2
  ↓
Host B
```

The switch does **not** send the frame to every port in VLAN 10.

---

## 4. Unknown-Unicast Forwarding

If the destination MAC is not present in the MAC address table:

```text
Host A → SW1
          ├──→ Host B
          ├──→ Host C
          └──→ Host D
```

The switch floods the frame within the applicable VLAN, excluding the ingress port and paths that are not eligible for normal forwarding.

This is called **unknown-unicast flooding**.

Once the destination MAC is learned, subsequent traffic can normally use direct known-unicast forwarding.

---

## 5. Same-Port Filtering

A particularly important Layer-2 behavior occurs when the destination MAC is known on the **same interface where the frame arrived**.

Example:

```text
Host A
  │
  │ Gi0/1
  ↓
Switch
```

MAC table:

```text
AAAA.AAAA.AAAA → Gi0/1, VLAN 10
```

If the switch receives a frame on Gi0/1 destined for `AAAA.AAAA.AAAA`, it does not send the frame back out Gi0/1.

The switch **filters/drops** the frame rather than forwarding it back onto the same ingress segment.

### Why?

The destination is already reachable through the same Layer-2 segment from which the frame arrived. Sending the frame back out the same port would be unnecessary.

---

## 6. Forwarding Is Primarily Hardware-Based

Modern enterprise switches perform normal Layer-2 forwarding using dedicated switching hardware, primarily **ASICs (Application-Specific Integrated Circuits)**.

This allows switches to process traffic at very high rates without requiring the main CPU to make every individual forwarding decision.

```text
Frame
  ↓
ASIC
  ↓
MAC lookup
  ↓
Forward / Filter / Flood
  ↓
Egress port
```

### Important distinction

The **CPU is not normally involved in forwarding every data frame**.

The CPU may handle:

- control-plane protocols;
    
- management traffic;
    
- exceptional packets;
    
- certain punted traffic;
    
- software-based processing when required.
    

---

## 7. CAM and MAC Address Lookup

A switch maintains a **MAC address table**, historically implemented using **CAM (Content-Addressable Memory)** in switching hardware.

Conceptually:

```text
MAC Address        VLAN       Port
-------------------------------------
AAAA.AAAA.AAAA     10         Gi0/1
BBBB.BBBB.BBBB     10         Gi0/2
CCCC.CCCC.CCCC     20         Gi0/3
```

The lookup considers the destination MAC in the context of the VLAN.

### CAM vs. TCAM

Do not confuse CAM with TCAM.

- **CAM** → commonly associated with exact-match lookups such as MAC addresses.
    
- **TCAM** → supports ternary matching (`0`, `1`, and `don't care`) and is commonly used for ACLs, QoS classification, routing-related lookups, and other policy-based functions.
    

The exact hardware architecture varies by Cisco platform and ASIC generation.

---

## 8. Store-and-Forward vs. Cut-Through

Switches can use different forwarding architectures.

|Mode|Basic Behavior|Advantage|Disadvantage|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Store-and-forward**|Receives the complete frame before forwarding|Can validate FCS and discard corrupted frames|Adds more latency|
|**Cut-through**|Begins forwarding before the complete frame is received|Lower latency|May forward a frame before its FCS can be validated|
|**Fragment-free**|Historical variation that waits for the initial portion of the frame|Reduced chance of forwarding collision fragments|Older/less relevant switching method|

### Store-and-Forward

The switch receives the complete Ethernet frame:

```text
Receive entire frame
        ↓
Check FCS
        ↓
Forward if valid
```

This allows the switch to detect an invalid FCS before transmitting the frame.

Modern Cisco enterprise switches generally use **store-and-forward switching**.

### Cut-Through

The switch begins forwarding before receiving the entire frame.

Conceptually:

```text
Receive enough information
        ↓
Determine forwarding decision
        ↓
Begin transmission
        ↓
Continue receiving the remainder
```

The primary advantage is lower forwarding latency.

## 9. Forwarding vs. Routing

|Feature|Layer-2 Forwarding|Layer-3 Routing|
|---|---|---|
|Primary address|Destination MAC|Destination IP|
|Main table|MAC address table|Routing table / FIB|
|Unit|Ethernet frame|IP packet|
|Typical device|Layer-2 switch|Router / Layer-3 switch|
|Broadcast domain|Remains within VLAN|Routed boundary stops L2 broadcast|
|Decision|Which Layer-2 port?|Which next hop/interface?|

### Example

```text
Host A
VLAN 10
  │
  ↓
L2 Switch
  │
  ↓
Default Gateway
  │
  ↓
L3 Routing
  │
  ↓
VLAN 20
  │
  ↓
Host B
```

Layer-2 forwarding gets the frame to the router/L3 switch.

Layer-3 routing then determines where the IP packet should go.

The packet is subsequently encapsulated in a **new Ethernet frame** for the next Layer-2 segment.

---

## 10. Forwarding and VLAN Boundaries

Layer-2 forwarding does not normally move a frame directly from one VLAN to another.

Example:

```text
VLAN 10                 VLAN 20

Host A                   Host B
  │                        │
  └────── L2 Switch ───────┘
```

A Layer-2 switch does not simply forward a VLAN 10 frame into VLAN 20.

Inter-VLAN communication requires a **Layer-3 routing function**.

```text
VLAN 10
   ↓
Layer-3 Gateway
   ↓
VLAN 20
```

---

## 11. Forwarding vs. Flooding vs. Filtering

These three behaviors are closely related:

### Forwarding

Destination MAC is known on another eligible port.

```text
Known destination
      ↓
Forward to specific port
```

### Flooding

Destination is unknown, or the frame is a broadcast/multicast that is being flooded.

```text
Unknown/broadcast
      ↓
Flood within VLAN
```

### Filtering

The switch determines that the frame should not be transmitted through the candidate egress interface.

Common example:

```text
Destination MAC is learned on ingress port
      ↓
Filter/drop
```

A useful mental model is:

```text
             Frame received
                    ↓
             Learn source MAC
                    ↓
          Look up destination MAC
                    ↓
        ┌───────────┼───────────┐
        ↓           ↓           ↓
      Known       Unknown    Broadcast/
      other       unicast    multicast
       port          ↓           ↓
        ↓          Flood     Flood /
    Forward                   constrain
        │
        └───────────┐
                    ↓
              Egress decision
                    ↓
               Transmit
```

## 12. Forwarding Failures / Troubleshooting

### 12.1 Missing MAC Entry

If the destination MAC is not learned:

```text
Destination unknown
       ↓
Unknown-unicast flooding
       ↓
Destination eventually responds
       ↓
MAC is learned
       ↓
Known-unicast forwarding
```

A missing MAC entry is therefore not necessarily a connectivity failure.

---

### 12.2 MAC Aging

Dynamic MAC addresses eventually age out if they are not refreshed.

On many Cisco IOS platforms, the default dynamic MAC aging timer is **300 seconds (5 minutes)**, but the value is configurable and platform-dependent.

When an entry ages out:

```text
MAC entry expires
       ↓
Destination becomes unknown
       ↓
Next traffic may be flooded
       ↓
MAC is relearned
       ↓
Known-unicast forwarding resumes
```

---

### 12.3 MAC Flapping

If the same MAC address appears to move between different switch ports repeatedly, the switch may report **MAC address flapping**.

Possible causes include:

- Layer-2 loops;
    
- redundant connections;
    
- incorrect EtherChannel configuration;
    
- bridging problems;
    
- topology changes.
    

MAC flapping is different from simply having a **CAM/MAC-table miss**.

---

### 12.4 STP Blocking

A port placed into a non-forwarding STP state does not participate in normal Layer-2 data forwarding.

Therefore, if a redundant path is blocked:

```text
SW1 ───────── SW2
 │             │
 └─────X───────┘
       STP
     blocking
```

the blocked path is intentionally not used for normal data forwarding.

This is expected behavior and prevents Layer-2 loops.

## 14. Exam Traps

### Trap 1 — "The switch changes the destination MAC"

**Incorrect.**

A normal Layer-2 switch forwards the Ethernet frame based on its destination MAC. It does not rewrite the destination MAC simply because it forwards the frame.

> Layer-3 routing is different: the original Ethernet frame is removed and a new Layer-2 frame is created for the next hop.

---

### Trap 2 — "If the destination is unknown, the frame is dropped"

**Incorrect.**

For normal Ethernet switching, an unknown unicast is generally **flooded within the VLAN**.

---

### Trap 3 — "A known MAC always means forward"

**Not necessarily.**

If the destination MAC is learned on the **same ingress port**, the switch filters the frame rather than sending it back out that port.

---

### Trap 4 — "Forwarding happens only after MAC learning"

**Incorrect.**

The switch can learn the source MAC and independently make a forwarding decision for the destination.

If the destination is unknown, the switch floods it.

---

### Trap 5 — "CAM and TCAM are the same"

**Incorrect.**

- **CAM** → exact-match lookups such as MAC addresses.
    
- **TCAM** → ternary/policy-oriented lookups such as ACL matching and other classification/routing functions.
    

---

### Trap 6 — "Every frame is processed by the switch CPU"

**Incorrect.**

Normal data-plane forwarding is primarily performed in dedicated switching hardware/ASICs.

---

### Trap 7 — "Store-and-forward and cut-through are routing methods"

**Incorrect.**

They describe **Layer-2 switching/forwarding architectures**, specifically when a switch begins transmitting a received frame.

---

### Trap 8 — "STP-blocked ports are used if the primary path is busy"

**Incorrect.**

STP places redundant paths into a non-forwarding state. They do not dynamically become forwarding simply because another path is busy.

A topology change or STP recalculation is required for a blocked path to become active.

## 15. Quick Review Table

|Step|Action|
|---|---|
|**1. Receive**|Receive frame on ingress interface|
|**2. Learn**|Source MAC → ingress port + VLAN|
|**3. Lookup**|Search destination MAC + VLAN|
|**4. Known / other port**|Forward to that port|
|**5. Known / ingress port**|Filter/drop|
|**6. Unknown unicast**|Flood within VLAN|
|**7. Broadcast**|Flood within VLAN|
|**8. Multicast**|Flood or constrain according to multicast state/features|
|**9. Hardware**|Normal forwarding handled primarily by ASICs|
|**10. Egress**|Transmit with appropriate Layer-2 encapsulation|

## 16. One-Minute Exam Summary

Remember the Layer-2 forwarding pipeline:

```text
RECEIVE
   ↓
LEARN SOURCE MAC
   ↓
LOOK UP DESTINATION MAC + VLAN
   ↓
┌──────────────────────────────────┐
│ Known → Forward                  │
│ Same ingress port → Filter      │
│ Unknown → Flood                  │
│ Broadcast → Flood               │
│ Multicast → Flood/constrain     │
└──────────────────────────────────┘
```

### The four words to remember

> **Learn → Lookup → Forward → Flood/Filter**

And the key distinction:

> **Forwarding sends a known destination toward a specific egress port; flooding sends traffic to multiple eligible ports within the VLAN; filtering prevents unnecessary forwarding.**
