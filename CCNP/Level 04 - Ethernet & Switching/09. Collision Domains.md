# Collision Domains

> **Level 04 · Ethernet & Switching** — CCNP Enterprise (ENCOR 350-401)

---

## 1. Overview

A **collision domain** is a Layer-2 network segment in which devices share a transmission medium and, in **half-duplex Ethernet**, can potentially interfere with one another by transmitting at the same time.

Collision domains were especially important with:

- Ethernet hubs;
    
- shared coaxial Ethernet;
    
- half-duplex Ethernet links;
    
- CSMA/CD.
    

Modern switched Ethernet normally operates in **full-duplex mode**, where collisions do not occur.

> **Key idea:** Switches divide traditional collision domains, while full-duplex operation eliminates the possibility of collisions on the link.

---

## 2. Where Do Collisions Come From?

Collisions occur when multiple devices share a common transmission medium and transmit simultaneously.

### Shared Ethernet

```text
       Shared Medium
────────────────────────────
   │          │          │
 Host A     Host B     Host C
   │          │          │
   └──────────┴──────────┘
          Collision
```

If Host A and Host B transmit at the same time:

```text
Host A ───────┐
              ├── Collision
Host B ───────┘
```

The transmitted frames interfere with each other.

In traditional half-duplex Ethernet, **CSMA/CD** detects the collision and causes the stations to back off and retransmit.

---

## 3. Hubs and Collision Domains

A **hub** is a Layer-1 device.

It does not examine destination MAC addresses or make Layer-2 forwarding decisions.

Instead, when a hub receives a signal, it repeats it out its other ports.

Therefore:

> **All devices connected to a hub share one collision domain.**

Example:

```text
             Hub
        ┌─────┼─────┐
        │     │     │
       PC1   PC2   PC3
```

PC1, PC2, and PC3 all share the same collision domain.

If two devices transmit simultaneously, their transmissions can collide.

---

## 4. Switches and Collision Domains

A Layer-2 switch separates collision domains by port.

Example:

```text
             Switch
        ┌─────┼─────┐
        │     │     │
       PC1   PC2   PC3
      Gi0/1 Gi0/2 Gi0/3
```

Conceptually:

```text
PC1 ↔ Gi0/1 → separate Layer-2 segment
PC2 ↔ Gi0/2 → separate Layer-2 segment
PC3 ↔ Gi0/3 → separate Layer-2 segment
```

For classic exam questions:

> **Each switch port represents a separate collision domain.**

This is often called **microsegmentation**.

---

## 5. Full-Duplex Ethernet

Modern Ethernet links are normally **full duplex**.

Full duplex means:

```text
Device A  ⇄  Device B

Transmit ─────────────→
Receive  ←─────────────
```

Both devices can transmit and receive simultaneously.

Because each direction has its own transmission path:

> **A full-duplex Ethernet link cannot have an Ethernet collision.**

Therefore, CSMA/CD is not used on a normal full-duplex switched Ethernet link.

### Important distinction

Do not say:

> "Full duplex creates zero collision domains."

A better statement is:

> **Full-duplex operation eliminates the possibility of collisions on that Ethernet link.**

The physical/Layer-2 segment still exists; it simply does not experience CSMA/CD collisions.

---

## 6. CSMA/CD

**CSMA/CD** stands for:

> **Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection**

It was used with traditional shared/half-duplex Ethernet.

### 6.1 Carrier Sense

A station listens to determine whether the medium is currently being used.

```text
Is the medium busy?
       ↓
    Yes → Wait
       ↓
    No → Transmit
```

---

### 6.2 Multiple Access

Multiple stations share the same physical medium.

```text
PC1 ─┐
PC2 ─┼── Shared Ethernet
PC3 ─┘
```

---

### 6.3 Collision Detection

If two stations transmit simultaneously:

```text
PC1 ──────┐
          ├── Collision
PC2 ──────┘
```

The stations detect the collision and perform the appropriate backoff procedure.

---

### 6.4 Binary Exponential Backoff

After a collision, stations wait for a pseudo-random period before retransmitting.

Repeated collisions increase the possible backoff range.

Conceptually:

```text
Collision
   ↓
Random backoff
   ↓
Retransmit
   ↓
Collision again?
   ↓
Larger backoff range
```

---

## 7. Collision Domains vs. Broadcast Domains

This is one of the most important distinctions.

|Feature|Collision Domain|Broadcast Domain|
|---|---|---|
|Main concern|Frame collisions|Broadcast propagation|
|Relevant technology|Shared/half-duplex Ethernet|Layer-2 VLANs|
|Traditional boundary|Switch/bridge|Router/Layer-3 boundary|
|Hub|One shared collision domain|Does not divide broadcast domain|
|Switch|Separates collision domains by port|One broadcast domain per VLAN|
|Full duplex|Collisions impossible|Broadcasts still exist|
|Main protocol concept|CSMA/CD|Ethernet broadcast flooding|

---

## 8. Hub vs. Switch

### Hub

```text
          Hub
       ┌───┼───┐
       │   │   │
      PC1 PC2 PC3

Collision Domains = 1
Broadcast Domains = 1
```

All hosts share the same collision domain.

---

### Switch

```text
             Switch
        ┌─────┼─────┐
        │     │     │
       PC1   PC2   PC3

Collision Domains:
Gi0/1 → separate
Gi0/2 → separate
Gi0/3 → separate
```

With one VLAN:

```text
Broadcast Domains = 1
```

With multiple VLANs:

```text
VLAN 10 → Broadcast Domain 1
VLAN 20 → Broadcast Domain 2
VLAN 30 → Broadcast Domain 3
```

## 9. Collision Domains in Modern Ethernet

For CCNP exam purposes, remember the traditional rule:

> **One switch port = one collision domain.**

However, modern Ethernet requires an important qualification.

If the switch port is operating at full duplex:

```text
Switch ───────── Host
       Full Duplex

Collisions = impossible
```

Therefore, collision domains are now primarily useful for understanding:

- legacy Ethernet;
    
- hubs;
    
- half-duplex operation;
    
- duplex mismatches;
    
- historical networking concepts;
    
- exam questions involving CSMA/CD.
    

---

## 10. Duplex Mismatch

A **duplex mismatch** occurs when one side of an Ethernet link operates in full duplex while the other operates in half duplex.

Example:

```text
Switch                  Device
Full Duplex             Half Duplex
     │                       │
     └──────── Ethernet ─────┘
```

This can cause:

- collisions;
    
- late collisions;
    
- FCS errors;
    
- input/output errors;
    
- poor performance;
    
- retransmissions.
    

### Important

Modern Ethernet links normally use **auto-negotiation**.

Do not manually force `duplex full` unless there is a specific design/troubleshooting reason and both sides are configured consistently.

### Check Interface Errors

```cisco

```

Look for counters such as:

- CRC;
    
- frame errors;
    
- collisions;
    
- late collisions;

## 12. Legacy Collision-Domain Counting

Classic networking questions may ask:

> "A 24-port switch has 24 connected devices. How many collision domains?"

Answer:

> **24 collision domains.**

Each switch port separates the connected device/segment from the others.

Example:

```text
24-Port Switch
      │
 ┌────┴────┐
 │ 24 ports│
 └─────────┘

Collision Domains = 24
```

### Another example

A switch has:

- 12 host-facing ports;
    
- 2 uplink ports.
    

Classic counting:

```text
12 access ports
+
2 uplink ports
──────────────
14 collision domains
```

This is the traditional exam model.

## 13. EtherChannel Consideration

EtherChannel combines multiple physical Ethernet links into one logical port-channel.

```text
       SW1
      ╱    ╲
     ╱      ╲
   Gi0/1   Gi0/2
     ╲      ╱
      ╲    ╱
       SW2
```

These physical member links operate independently at the physical level, but the EtherChannel is treated as a **single logical Layer-2 interface** for forwarding and control purposes.

For modern full-duplex Ethernet, collision counting is generally not meaningful because collisions are not expected on properly operating member links.

---

## 14. Design Impact

### Full-Duplex Switched Links

A dedicated full-duplex switch port provides:

- simultaneous transmit/receive;
    
- no CSMA/CD;
    
- no Ethernet collisions;
    
- predictable link utilization.
    

```text
Host
  ⇅
Switch
Full Duplex
```

---

### Oversubscription Still Matters

Although a host may have a dedicated switch port, that does not guarantee unlimited end-to-end bandwidth.

Example:

```text
10 × 1-Gbps Access Ports
          ↓
       1-Gbps
        Uplink
```

The access ports are individually collision-free, but the uplink can become a **bandwidth bottleneck**.

Therefore:

> **Collision-domain separation does not eliminate bandwidth bottlenecks or oversubscription.**

## 15. Exam Traps

### Trap 1 — "A hub creates multiple collision domains"

**Incorrect.**

A hub creates/extends **one shared collision domain**.

---

### Trap 2 — "A switch creates one collision domain for the entire switch"

**Incorrect.**

For classic Ethernet terminology:

> **Each switch port = separate collision domain.**

---

### Trap 3 — "Full duplex means there is no collision domain"

**Imprecise.**

Better:

> **Full-duplex Ethernet eliminates the possibility of collisions on the link.**

---

### Trap 4 — "CSMA/CD is required on modern full-duplex Ethernet"

**Incorrect.**

CSMA/CD is associated with **half-duplex/shared Ethernet**.

Modern full-duplex switched Ethernet does not use collision detection.

---

### Trap 5 — "A router creates one collision domain for the whole router"

**Incorrect.**

Each Ethernet interface connects to its own Layer-2 segment.

---

### Trap 6 — "Collision domain = broadcast domain"

**Incorrect.**

They solve different problems:

```text
Collision Domain
      ↓
Potential frame collisions

Broadcast Domain
      ↓
Broadcast propagation
```

---

### Trap 7 — "Switch ports always have collisions"

**Incorrect.**

On properly operating full-duplex Ethernet, collisions are not expected.

---

### Trap 8 — "Manually configure full duplex everywhere"

**Incorrect.**

Modern Ethernet normally uses **auto-negotiation**. Manual settings should be used only when required and consistently configured on both sides.

---

## 16. Troubleshooting Workflow

If you see unexpected collisions or interface errors:

```text
1. Check interface status
        ↓
2. Check speed and duplex
        ↓
3. Check both sides of the link
        ↓
4. Look for duplex mismatch
        ↓
5. Check CRC / FCS errors
        ↓
6. Check collision / late-collision counters
        ↓
7. Check cabling/transceiver
        ↓
8. Check for legacy hub/half-duplex equipment
```

Useful command:

```cisco
show interfaces GigabitEthernet0/1
```

## 18. One-Minute Exam Summary

Remember:

```text
HUB
 ↓
One shared collision domain
 ↓
CSMA/CD may be required

SWITCH
 ↓
Separates collision domains per port
 ↓
Dedicated Layer-2 segments

FULL DUPLEX
 ↓
Transmit + receive simultaneously
 ↓
No Ethernet collisions
 ↓
No CSMA/CD
```

### Classic exam rule

> **Hub = one collision domain.**

> **Switch = one collision domain per port.**

> **Full duplex = collisions cannot occur.**

And do not confuse:

> **Collision domain → concerns frame collisions/medium sharing.**

> **Broadcast domain → concerns Layer-2 broadcast propagation and is normally defined by the VLAN.**
