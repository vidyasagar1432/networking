# Broadcast Domains

> **Level 04 · Ethernet & Switching** — CCNP Enterprise (ENCOR 350-401)

---

## 1. Overview

A **broadcast domain** is the set of Layer-2 devices/interfaces that can receive a Layer-2 **broadcast frame** within the same logical network.

An Ethernet broadcast uses the destination MAC address:

```text
FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF
```

In a typical switched Ethernet network:

> **One VLAN = one Layer-2 broadcast domain.**

A **Layer-3 boundary** separates broadcast domains. Routers and Layer-3 switch interfaces do not forward Layer-2 broadcasts from one VLAN/subnet into another by default.

Broadcast-domain design is important for:

- scalability;
    
- traffic containment;
    
- network segmentation;
    
- troubleshooting;
    
- Layer-2 failure containment.
    

---

## 2. What Is Inside a Broadcast Domain?

Consider:

```text
             VLAN 10
       ┌─────────────────┐
       │      Switch     │
       └─────────────────┘
          │     │     │
         PC1   PC2   PC3
```

All three hosts are in VLAN 10.

If PC1 sends:

```text
Destination MAC:
FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF
```

the switch floods the broadcast to the other eligible ports in **VLAN 10**.

Therefore:

```text
PC1 ──┐
PC2 ──┼── VLAN 10 Broadcast Domain
PC3 ──┘
```

---

## 3. Broadcast Domain Boundaries

|Device / Feature|Effect on Broadcast Domain|
|---|---|
|**Hub**|Does not divide the broadcast domain|
|**Layer-2 Switch**|One broadcast domain per VLAN|
|**Router**|Separates broadcast domains|
|**Layer-3 Switch / SVI**|Provides a Layer-3 boundary between VLANs|
|**VLAN**|Defines a Layer-2 broadcast domain|
|**Routed Interface**|Creates a Layer-3 boundary|
|**Trunk**|Carries multiple VLANs; does not merge their broadcast domains|

### Important

A trunk can carry VLAN 10 and VLAN 20 simultaneously:

```text
          Trunk
SW1 ================= SW2
    VLAN 10 + VLAN 20
```

But the broadcasts remain separate:

```text
VLAN 10 broadcast
       ↓
VLAN 10 only

VLAN 20 broadcast
       ↓
VLAN 20 only
```

A trunk **transports multiple broadcast domains; it does not merge them**.

---

## 4. Router / Layer-3 Boundary

Routers and Layer-3 interfaces separate Layer-2 broadcast domains.

Example:

```text
VLAN 10                  VLAN 20
────────                 ────────
PC1                      PC2
 │                        │
 └─────── L3 Switch ──────┘
              │
          Routing boundary
```

A broadcast originating in VLAN 10 does not normally get forwarded into VLAN 20 as a Layer-2 broadcast.

Instead, Layer-3 communication between the VLANs requires routing.

```text
VLAN 10
  ↓
SVI / Router
  ↓
Layer-3 routing
  ↓
VLAN 20
```

## 5. What Uses Broadcast?

Several protocols and services can generate broadcast traffic.

### ARP

An IPv4 host can use an ARP request to discover the MAC address associated with an IPv4 address on the local subnet.

Example:

```text
Who has 10.1.10.20?

Destination:
FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF
```

The broadcast is flooded throughout the local VLAN.

---

### DHCP

A DHCP client may initially send a **DHCP Discover** as a broadcast because it does not yet know the DHCP server's address.

```text
Client
  ↓
DHCP Discover
  ↓
Broadcast
  ↓
Local VLAN
```

A DHCP relay can forward the request across a Layer-3 boundary toward a DHCP server.

---

### Other Protocols

Depending on the protocol, version, and configuration, broadcast traffic can also be generated by services such as:

- NetBIOS/NBNS;
    
- some service-discovery mechanisms;
    
- some legacy Windows/network protocols;
    
- certain routing protocols or protocol versions.
    

> **Important:** Not every network protocol uses broadcast. Many modern protocols use unicast, multicast, or other mechanisms instead.

## 6. Why Broadcast-Domain Size Matters

### 6.1 Scalability

As the number of devices in a broadcast domain increases, broadcast traffic reaches more devices.

```text
Small VLAN
   ↓
10 hosts
   ↓
Broadcast reaches fewer devices

Large VLAN
   ↓
500 hosts
   ↓
Broadcast reaches many more devices
```

A large Layer-2 domain can therefore increase unnecessary traffic processing.

---

### 6.2 Host Processing

A broadcast frame is delivered to multiple devices.

Each receiving device's NIC and operating system must determine whether the traffic requires processing.

High broadcast rates can therefore increase:

- CPU utilization;
    
- interrupt processing;
    
- network traffic;
    
- unnecessary packet handling.
    

---

### 6.3 Failure Containment

A broadcast storm inside one VLAN can affect the entire broadcast domain.

Smaller Layer-2 domains help contain the impact.

```text
VLAN 10
Broadcast storm
      ↓
Contained within VLAN 10
```

The Layer-3 boundary prevents the Layer-2 broadcast from automatically propagating into another VLAN.

---

### 6.4 Security and Visibility

Broadcast traffic is delivered to multiple devices within the broadcast domain.

This can expose information such as:

- ARP information;
    
- host/service discovery;
    
- network configuration information.
    

However:

> **A broadcast domain is not a security boundary by itself.**

Proper security requires controls such as:

- VLAN segmentation;
    
- ACLs;
    
- port security;
    
- DHCP snooping;
    
- Dynamic ARP Inspection;
    
- private VLANs;
    
- Layer-3 firewall policies.
    

---

## 7. Broadcast Storms

A **broadcast storm** occurs when excessive broadcast traffic consumes network resources.

A common cause is a Layer-2 loop.

```text
        SW1
       /   \
      /     \
    SW2 ─── SW3
```

Without effective loop prevention, broadcast frames can circulate repeatedly.

Potential effects include:

- excessive bandwidth consumption;
    
- MAC-table instability;
    
- high switch CPU utilization;
    
- packet loss;
    
- network-wide connectivity problems.
    

### Main protection mechanisms

**STP/RSTP/MST**

> Prevent Layer-2 loops by controlling the active topology.

**Storm Control**

> Limits excessive supported traffic categories entering an interface.

These solve different problems:

```text
STP
 ↓
Prevents Layer-2 loops

Storm Control
 ↓
Limits excessive traffic
```

Storm control should not be considered a replacement for STP.

## 8. How to Reduce Broadcast-Domain Size

### 8.1 VLAN Segmentation

The most common method is to divide a large Layer-2 network into multiple VLANs.

```text
Before:

One large VLAN
────────────────────
PC PC PC PC PC PC PC


After:

VLAN 10
────────────
PC PC PC

VLAN 20
────────────
PC PC PC

VLAN 30
────────────
PC PC PC
```

Each VLAN is a separate broadcast domain.

---

### 8.2 Inter-VLAN Routing

Once VLANs are separated, Layer-3 routing provides communication between them.

```text
VLAN 10
   │
   ↓
L3 Switch / Router
   │
   ↓
VLAN 20
```

This allows controlled communication between broadcast domains.

---

### 8.3 Routed Access

In larger campus designs, access switches can use routed uplinks rather than extending large Layer-2 VLANs throughout the network.

Conceptually:

```text
Access SW1 ──┐
             │
Access SW2 ──┼── L3 Core
             │
Access SW3 ──┘
```

This can significantly reduce Layer-2 domain size and improve failure containment.

---

## 9. VLAN vs. Broadcast Domain

For normal Ethernet switching:

```text
VLAN 10 = Broadcast Domain 10

VLAN 20 = Broadcast Domain 20

VLAN 30 = Broadcast Domain 30
```

A switch can therefore contain multiple broadcast domains:

```text
                 Switch
        ┌─────────┼─────────┐
        ↓         ↓         ↓
     VLAN 10   VLAN 20   VLAN 30
        ↓         ↓         ↓
      BD 10     BD 20     BD 30
```

### Important

The switch itself does not create a single broadcast domain across all its ports.

**Each VLAN represents a separate Layer-2 broadcast domain.**

---

## 10. Broadcast Domain vs. Collision Domain

This is a classic CCNP/CCNA distinction.

|Feature|Broadcast Domain|Collision Domain|
|---|---|---|
|Concern|Layer-2 broadcast propagation|Ethernet frame contention/collisions|
|Example traffic|ARP broadcast|Simultaneous transmission|
|Primary boundary|VLAN / Layer-3 boundary|Switch port / bridge segment|
|Router|Separates broadcast domains|Separates Layer-2 segments|
|Layer-2 switch|One broadcast domain per VLAN|Typically one collision domain per port|
|Hub|Extends one broadcast domain|All connected devices share one collision domain|
|Modern full-duplex Ethernet|Broadcasts still exist|CSMA/CD collisions are not used|

### Modern Ethernet Note

In modern switched **full-duplex Ethernet**, devices can transmit and receive simultaneously without CSMA/CD collision detection.

Therefore, collision domains are primarily a **legacy Ethernet concept**, while broadcast domains remain highly relevant in modern networks.

## 11. Private VLANs

**Private VLANs (PVLANs)** provide additional Layer-2 isolation within a primary VLAN.

For example:

```text
Primary VLAN
──────────────────────────
 │        │         │
 ↓        ↓         ↓
Host A   Host B    Gateway
 │        │
 X────────X
   isolated
```

PVLANs can prevent certain hosts from communicating directly with each other while still allowing communication with permitted destinations such as a promiscuous port.

### Important distinction

> **PVLANs provide Layer-2 isolation within a VLAN; they are not simply additional ordinary broadcast domains.**

---

## 12. IGMP Snooping

**IGMP snooping** helps control IPv4 multicast forwarding at the Layer-2 switch.

Without appropriate multicast state:

```text
Multicast source
      ↓
   Switch
  ┌─┼─┼─┐
  ↓ ↓ ↓ ↓
 H1 H2 H3 H4
```

With IGMP snooping:

```text
Multicast source
      ↓
   Switch
      │
      ↓
  Interested
  receivers
```

### Important

IGMP snooping:

- reduces unnecessary multicast forwarding;
    
- does **not** reduce the size of the broadcast domain;
    
- does **not** replace VLAN segmentation.

## 15. Exam Traps

### Trap 1 — "A switch is one broadcast domain"

**Incorrect.**

A Layer-2 switch normally has **one broadcast domain per VLAN**.

```text
VLAN 10 → Broadcast Domain 10
VLAN 20 → Broadcast Domain 20
```

---

### Trap 2 — "A trunk merges VLANs"

**Incorrect.**

A trunk carries multiple VLANs but keeps their Layer-2 broadcast domains logically separate.

---

### Trap 3 — "A router forwards broadcasts between VLANs"

**Incorrect by default.**

Routers and Layer-3 interfaces provide a boundary for Layer-2 broadcasts.

Special mechanisms can intentionally relay certain broadcast-derived protocols, such as DHCP through a relay, but that is not ordinary Layer-2 broadcast forwarding.

---

### Trap 4 — "An SVI creates a broadcast domain"

**Imprecise.**

The **VLAN** is the Layer-2 broadcast domain.

The **SVI provides the Layer-3 interface/gateway** for that VLAN and enables routing between VLANs.

---

### Trap 5 — "VRF reduces the broadcast domain"

**Incorrect.**

VRF provides separate Layer-3 routing/forwarding tables.

It is a segmentation mechanism, but it does not itself divide a Layer-2 broadcast domain.

---

### Trap 6 — "IGMP snooping reduces the broadcast domain"

**Incorrect.**

IGMP snooping reduces unnecessary **multicast forwarding**.

VLANs are what define normal Layer-2 broadcast domains.

---

### Trap 7 — "Private VLANs create normal separate broadcast domains"

**Incorrect.**

PVLANs provide additional Layer-2 isolation within a primary VLAN.

---

### Trap 8 — "Broadcast and collision domains are the same"

**Incorrect.**

```text
Broadcast domain
    ↓
VLAN / Layer-3 boundary

Collision domain
    ↓
Layer-2 segment / switch port
```

Modern full-duplex Ethernet eliminates normal CSMA/CD collisions.

## 16. Troubleshooting Workflow

When investigating broadcast-related problems:

```text
1. Identify the affected VLAN
        ↓
2. Check VLAN membership
        ↓
3. Check trunk VLAN allowance
        ↓
4. Check STP topology
        ↓
5. Look for Layer-2 loops
        ↓
6. Check broadcast traffic levels
        ↓
7. Check storm-control configuration
        ↓
8. Check multicast/IGMP snooping if applicable
        ↓
9. Check Layer-3 boundaries and routing
```

Useful commands:

```cisco
show vlan brief
show interfaces trunk
show spanning-tree
show interfaces counters
show storm-control
show ip interface brief
show ip route
```

## 18. One-Minute Exam Summary

Remember:

> **A broadcast domain is the Layer-2 area in which a broadcast can propagate.**

The most important relationship is:

```text
             VLAN
              ↓
      Broadcast Domain
              ↓
     Broadcast can propagate
       within that VLAN
```

Multiple VLANs create multiple broadcast domains:

```text
VLAN 10 ──→ Broadcast Domain 10

VLAN 20 ──→ Broadcast Domain 20

VLAN 30 ──→ Broadcast Domain 30
```

A Layer-3 boundary separates them:

```text
VLAN 10
   ↓
L3 Switch / Router
   ↓
VLAN 20
```

### Core exam rule

> **VLANs define Layer-2 broadcast domains; Layer-3 interfaces separate those domains.**

And remember the classic comparison:

> **Broadcast domain = VLAN/L3 boundary.**  
> **Collision domain = Layer-2 segment/switch-port concept.**
