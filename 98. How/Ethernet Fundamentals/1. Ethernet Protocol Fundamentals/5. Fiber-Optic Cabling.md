==========

Fiber-optic cabling is widely used for high-speed Ethernet links over relatively long distances. It uses glass or plastic fiber as a medium through which light is "guided" to the other end of the link. The fiber-optic cable itself has several layers made from different materials and having different functions. The most important layer is the **core**, which is the very center of the cable. A light source, called transmitter or Tx, shines a light into the core. The core itself is surrounded by optical material called the **cladding** that keeps the light in the core using an optical technique called *total internal reflection*. Together the **cladding** and **core** create the environment to allow transmission of light over the cable.

![Fiber Optic Cable Components](../../images/www.networkacademy.io/sites/default/files/inline-images/Fiber-Optic-Cable-Components.png)

Figure 1. Fiber Optic Cable Components

Figure 1 shows a simplified view of typical fiber-optic components. You can see that the *core* and *cladding* are at the very center of the cable. The outer *coating* layer protects the interior and makes the cable stronger and easier to install and manage. Depending on the cable type, the outer layer could be made of a few additional sublayers such as *buffer* and *strengthener*.

**KEY TOPIC** Depending primarily on the diameter of the core, fiber-optics are separated into two main types: **single-mode fiber (SMF)** and **multimode fiber (MMF)**.

Figure 2 shows the difference in the diameter of the core between the two types. Typically, the core of single-mode fiber is up to five times smaller than the core of multimode fiber optic.

Of course, there are other differences between the two types, which we are going to look at next.

## Multi-Mode Fiber Optics

Due to its bigger core, some of the light beams may travel a direct route, whereas others bounce off the cladding as shown in Figure 3. These alternate paths cause the different groups of light beams, referred to as modes, to arrive separately at the end of the link. Because of this, the strength of the light is reduced over long distances.

![Multi-Mode Fiber Optics](../../images/www.networkacademy.io/sites/default/files/inline-images/Multi-Mode-Fiber-Optic_0.gif)

Figure 3. Multi-Mode Fiber Optics

Due to the large core size of multimode fiber, some low-cost light sources like LEDs (light-emitting diodes) and VCSELs (vertical-cavity surface-emitting lasers) are typically used. Because of this, transmission system costs (transmitters and receivers) are lower than single-mode fiber. Typical light wavelengths used are 850 nm and 1300 nm.

In summary, multimode fiber gives high bandwidth at high speeds over medium distances (up to 1km) at a lower cost.

## Single-Mode Fiber Optics

Single-mode fiber cables have up to 5 times smaller diametral core than the multi-mode cables. It allows only one mode of light to propagate through the core as shown in Figure 4. Because of this, the number of light reflections is lower than of multimode fiber, so the signal can travel further.

![Single-Mode Fiber Optics (SMF)](../../images/www.networkacademy.io/sites/default/files/inline-images/Single-Mode-Fiber-Optic_0.gif)

Figure 4. Single-Mode Fiber Optics (SMF)

Single-mode fiber typically uses a laser or laser diodes to emit light into the core. Because of this, transmission system costs (transmitters and receivers) are higher than multimode fiber. Typical light wavelengths used are 1310 nm and 1550 nm. Single-mode fiber bandwidth is theoretically unlimited because it allows only one light mode to goes through at a time.

In summary, single-mode fiber gives unlimited bandwidth at very high speeds over long distances (up to 80km) at a high cost.

## Using Ethernet with fiber-optics

A typical Ethernet link with fiber needs two fiber cables, one for each direction, and two transceiver modules such as SFP or SFP+, as shown in Figure 5. Note that the transmitting end on one device connects to a cable that connects to receiving end on the other device and vice versa with the other cable. This concept is particularly important to know when the connection is not a direct cable between two devices but goes via structured cabling systems such as Optical Distribution Frames (ODF).

![Fiber-optic Ethernet link with Tx connect to Rx on each cable](../../images/www.networkacademy.io/sites/default/files/inline-images/Fiber-optic-Ethernet-link.gif)

Figure 5. Ethernet link with two Fiber Cables with Tx Connected to Rx on Each Cable

When the Ethernet link is not direct but is made of a few connected cables, it is very easy to cross the Rx/Tx ends and to connect the transmit side of one device to the transmit side on the other one.

## Single Mode vs Multimode Fiber

Although both types of optics are widely used, the differences between single-mode fiber cables and multimode fiber cables are still confusing for many network engineers. Let's answer the most common questions about them.

##### How can I differentiate single mode from multimode cable?

Single-mode cables are coated with yellow outer sheath, and multimode cables are coated either with orange or aqua jacket.

![Color sheath of fiber cables](../../images/www.networkacademy.io/sites/default/files/inline-images/Color-sheath-of-fiber-cables.png)

Figure 6. Color sheath of fiber cables

##### Which one is better - single-mode or multimode?

Each type has its own advantages and disadvantages, so there is no better one. Choosing the best fit for your network is the best. The leading factor to consider is the distance required. If the distances are longer than 500m you chose single-mode, for data centers usually multimode cables are used. Costs and upgradability are other important factors to consider.

## Fiber Connector Types

Fiber-optic cables use different types of connectors depending on the application. The connector is the component at the end of a fiber cable that plugs into a transceiver or patch panel. Knowing the common connector types is important for the CCNA exam and for real-world networking.

**LC Connector (Lucent Connector)** — This is the most common connector type used with SFP and SFP+ transceivers. It has a small 1.25 mm ferrule and a latch mechanism similar to an RJ45 connector. Because of its small size, LC connectors are ideal for high-density environments like data centers.

**SC Connector (Subscriber Connector)** — This connector uses a 2.5 mm ferrule and a push-pull coupling mechanism. It was very popular in older Ethernet networks and is still widely used for fiber patch panels. SC connectors are larger than LC connectors.

**ST Connector (Straight Tip)** — This connector uses a 2.5 mm ferrule with a bayonet-style twist-lock mechanism. It was common in older networks and is still found in some industrial environments. ST connectors are not typically used in new deployments.

**FC Connector (Ferrule Connector)** — This connector uses a 2.5 mm ferrule with a threaded screw-on mechanism. It provides a very secure connection and is commonly used in high-vibration environments and for test equipment.

**MPO/MTP Connector** — This is a multi-fiber connector that can hold 12 or 24 fibers in a single connector body. It is used for high-speed parallel optics like 40GBASE-SR4 and 100GBASE-SR10.

| Connector | Ferrule Size | Common Use |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| LC | 1.25 mm | SFP/SFP+ transceivers, data centers |
| SC | 2.5 mm | Patch panels, enterprise networks |
| ST | 2.5 mm | Legacy networks, industrial |
| FC | 2.5 mm | Test equipment, high-vibration |
| MPO/MTP | Multi-fiber | 40G/100G parallel optics |

## Transceiver Form Factors

A transceiver (short for transmitter-receiver) is the module that converts electrical signals from the switch or router into light signals for the fiber cable. Over the years, several form factors have been standardized.

**GBIC (Gigabit Interface Converter)** — This was the first standardized hot-swappable transceiver. It is large in size and supports speeds up to 1 Gbps. GBICs are now mostly obsolete but you may still find them in older equipment.

**SFP (Small Form-factor Pluggable)** — Also called Mini-GBIC, SFP is the most common transceiver type for speeds up to 1 Gbps. It is much smaller than GBIC and provides twice the port density. SFP supports both copper (1000BASE-T) and fiber (1000BASE-SX, 1000BASE-LX) interfaces.

**SFP+ (Enhanced Small Form-factor Pluggable)** — This is an enhanced version of SFP that supports speeds up to 10 Gbps. SFP+ has the same physical size as SFP, so SFP+ ports can usually accept SFP modules (but not the other way around).

**QSFP/QSFP+ (Quad Small Form-factor Pluggable)** — This transceiver supports 40 Gbps by aggregating four 10 Gbps channels. QSFP is larger than SFP+ and uses MPO/MTP multi-fiber connectors for fiber connections.

**QSFP28** — This is the 100 Gbps version of QSFP. It uses four 25 Gbps channels to achieve 100 Gbps. It has the same form factor as QSFP+.

| Form Factor | Speed | Typical Media |
|-------------|-------|---------------|
| GBIC | Up to 1 Gbps | Copper or fiber |
| SFP | Up to 1 Gbps | Copper or fiber |
| SFP+ | Up to 10 Gbps | Copper or fiber |
| QSFP+ | Up to 40 Gbps | Fiber (MPO) |
| QSFP28 | Up to 100 Gbps | Fiber (MPO) |

## Fiber-Optic Standards Summary

For the CCNA exam, you should know the following fiber-optic Ethernet standards.

**1000BASE-SX** — Uses multimode fiber (MMF) with LC connectors. Supports distances up to 550 meters depending on the fiber grade. Uses short-wavelength laser (850 nm). Commonly used in data centers and campus networks.

**1000BASE-LX** — Uses single-mode fiber (SMF) with LC connectors. Supports distances up to 5 km (or 10 km with enhanced optics). Uses long-wavelength laser (1310 nm). Designed for longer-haul connections.

**1000BASE-LH** — An extended-range version of 1000BASE-LX that supports distances up to 70 km.

**10GBASE-SR** — Uses multimode fiber with SFP+ connectors. Supports distances up to 400 meters. Uses 850 nm wavelength. The most common 10 Gbps fiber standard.

**10GBASE-LR** — Uses single-mode fiber with SFP+ connectors. Supports distances up to 10 km. Uses 1310 nm wavelength.

**10GBASE-ER** — Uses single-mode fiber with SFP+ connectors. Supports distances up to 40 km. Uses 1550 nm wavelength.

## Fiber Safety and Handling

Fiber-optic cables require careful handling. Here are some important points to remember.

**Don't look into the laser** — Fiber transceivers use lasers that can cause permanent eye damage. Never look directly into a fiber connector or transceiver port.

**Keep connectors clean** — Dust and dirt on fiber connectors can cause signal loss and damage the transceiver. Always use caps to protect unused connectors, and clean them with appropriate fiber-cleaning tools before use.

**Minimum bend radius** — Fiber cables have a minimum bend radius. Bending the cable tighter than this radius can damage the glass core and cause signal loss. Always follow the manufacturer's specifications.

**Don't pull on the cable** — When installing fiber cables, pull on the strength members (Kevlar or aramid yarn), not on the fiber itself.

## Summary

In this lesson, we covered everything you need to know about fiber-optic cabling for the CCNA exam.

- **Single-mode fiber (SMF)** has a small core, uses laser light, and supports very long distances (up to 80 km) at the highest speeds. It is identified by a yellow outer jacket.
- **Multimode fiber (MMF)** has a larger core, uses LED or VCSEL light sources, and supports medium distances (up to 1 km) at lower cost. It is identified by an orange or aqua outer jacket.
- A fiber-optic Ethernet link requires **two fiber cables** (one for each direction) and **two transceivers** (one on each end).
- Common **connector types** include LC (most common for SFP), SC (patch panels), and MPO (high-speed parallel optics).
- Common **transceiver form factors** include SFP (1 Gbps), SFP+ (10 Gbps), QSFP+ (40 Gbps), and QSFP28 (100 Gbps).
- Always handle fiber cables with care — **keep connectors clean**, respect the **minimum bend radius**, and **never look into the laser**.
